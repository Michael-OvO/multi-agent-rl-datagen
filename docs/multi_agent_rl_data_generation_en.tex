{\bfseries Abstract}\hspace{0.6em}This report presents a method, and one substrate carried through it end to end. The method takes an agentic task database that was never meant for multi-agent work and turns its single-agent tasks into training tasks that cannot be finished without collaboration --- without writing a new task, a new environment, or a new verifier. It asks four things of a database and nothing else: a verifier somebody else wrote, an installable world, ground truth per task, and an action surface that divides along role boundaries. Any database clearing those admits the method; Gaia2, the benchmark shipped with Meta's Agents Research Environments~\cite{meta2025are}, is the one this report runs, and \S\ref{sec:substrate} states plainly that it is the only one. Concretely, on Gaia2: read the gold write actions and the full initial app state of each scenario, and keep, via a state-provenance test, the scenarios in which a written argument value provably originated in another app. The roster comes from the apps the ground truth actually touched, rather than from padding a scenario out to look multi-agent. Each scenario is then wrapped in a permission partition: the Main agent holds no app tool at all and can only delegate, while the verifier and the ground truth stay on a side it cannot reach. Whether the scenario is done is still decided by Gaia2's own write-action verifier, and its scripted mode has no model in it.

\vspace{0.5em}
The cost structure of this method is lopsided, and that is what determines how it scales. Mining scenarios, deriving rosters, and rendering task directories are all static analysis with no model calls: 37 admitted scenarios spread into 148 rendered cells in one command and a few minutes. The bills come afterwards, and this report is mostly about what they bought. A full single-seed campaign over all 148 cells is reported in \S\ref{sec:results}, and its verdict is negative: the unconstrained control succeeds on 2 of 37 scenarios, every constrained arm lands within $0.035$ of it, and the acceptance criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} therefore certifies nothing --- a knob's effect cannot be read against a control that has already failed. The way up is consequently not more knobs and not more cells. It is a control that clears the floor: 49\% of the campaign's rollouts die on malformed tool calls or an environment stop, before coordination is on trial at all.

\section{Problem and Goal}

A task usable for reinforcement learning needs at least three parts: the instruction the model receives, the environment it can interact with, and the reward issued once the episode ends. Single-agent tasks are usually easy to verify --- check whether a file was created, whether a database row changed, whether the tests pass. Multi-agent tasks are harder, because we care about more than the end result: we want the model to hand subtasks to the right role and to move the necessary facts between roles.

There is an easy mistake here. Add a few roles and a group chat, and the task looks multi-agent. But if the Main agent still holds every tool, or if any sub-agent could finish the task alone, then the roles are set dressing and produce no new learning signal. Score the transcript on whether it \emph{looks} collaborative instead, and the reward becomes subjective and impossible to scale.

So this project narrows the goal: start from real tasks that already have a trustworthy verifier, then use permission and information constraints to make collaboration \emph{necessary} for finishing. The model must demonstrate coordination, but the reward is still decided solely by the end state.

Stated at the level it is meant to be reused at, that goal is a \textbf{method} rather than a dataset. No corpus of multi-agent traces with environments and free verifiers exists, so anyone building this data must manufacture the multi-agent structure themselves; the only real decision is \emph{which layer} they manufacture. Manufacture the verifier and the mistakes become invisible, because your own tests will agree with your own errors --- \S\ref{sec:invariant} is that failure, measured. Manufacture only \emph{constraints}, which change who may do what and never what counts as done, and the failure mode becomes a task that is too easy or too hard: visible, and priceable by the criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion}. The rest of this report is that method in four moves --- admit a database (\S\ref{sec:substrate}), mine its seams (\S\ref{sec:select}), constrain access (\S\ref{sec:wrap}), accept only the cells that land between the floor and the control (\S\ref{sec:criterion}) --- and one database, Gaia2, carried through all four.

\section{Capability Selection and Learning Order}

Multi-agent systems involve many capabilities. To keep the training signal legible, this design first picks the ones that can be verified from environment outcomes and that compose in layers. MAST groups common failures into system design, inter-agent misalignment, task verification and termination, and related classes~\cite{cemri2025mast}; MultiAgentBench shows that star, chain, tree and graph topologies impose different coordination demands~\cite{zhu2025multiagentbench}. At the data-construction stage, though, changing every factor at once is the wrong move.

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The first three capabilities in Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} are exercised directly by the current delivery. Parallel scheduling is the exception. It is listed because it belongs to the same family --- verifiable from environment state --- but this delivery does not cover it: the Main issues one \texttt{DELEGATE} per turn and \texttt{run\_specialist} returns before the next may start, so delegations block serially however wide the scenario's dependency graph is. Gaia2 does at least make the missing half cheap to imagine: its clock is simulated and the Main advances it explicitly with \texttt{WAIT}, so makespan is a quantity the environment already tracks. What is missing is a runtime that can issue two briefs at once, and a reward term that prices the difference. Until both exist, the dimension stays out of scope --- the same reason Theory of Mind is not listed as its own dimension below.

These capabilities suit layered training. The first layer asks only that the model identify the roles and delegate once, correctly. The second adds a cross-app dependency, so the Main must carry an upstream result downstream. The third hides the roles' capability documentation, forcing the model to work out who might know what. Only later come delegation budgets, more roles, and richer topologies. One clarification is due here: the third layer already implies Theory of Mind --- judging \emph{who might know what} is exactly modelling another role's knowledge state, and the failure criterion for ``cross-role communication'' in Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} is the same thing seen from the other side, where the Main assumes the sub-agent knows as much as it does and issues a delegation the sub-agent cannot act on. The reason Theory of Mind and dynamic recovery are still not separate dimensions is not that they are unimportant. It is that the current reward is a single terminal scalar and cannot attribute one failure to that link alone.

That limitation belongs to this design, not to the capability. By the rule of \S\ref{sec:invariant} --- the sin is the unmeasured verifier, not the language model --- attribution is not hopeless: a teacher model that reads the ledger and decides whether a failure lay in decomposition, routing or communication is entirely buildable. It is a self-designed judge, so it needs a validation study (agreement against human labels), not a prohibition. What is unacceptable is using one \emph{without measuring it}, which is precisely what v1 did with that one line of division. So the route is: the terminal scalar stays the only training reward, and the teacher model is used for \emph{diagnosis} and curriculum design; once its attribution accuracy has been measured, we can discuss whether it may enter the reward. This report has not done that step, so the table above lists only terminally verifiable capabilities.

\begin{quote}
\small
\noindent The suggested progression is: single-shot role routing $\rightarrow$ star + visible capability docs $\rightarrow$
star + role names only $\rightarrow$ limited budget and multi-level dependencies $\rightarrow$ chain/tree and runtime faults.
\end{quote}

The current implementation and experiments reach the third layer. The last two have a clear construction path, but each must be shown solvable and shown to add difficulty before it enters a training set.

\section{From Single-Agent Task to Collaborative Task}
\label{sec:wrap}

The method fits in one sentence: \emph{keep the task and the verifier; restructure only how agents reach the task.}

The original task already has a user instruction, an interactive environment, and a programmatic verifier. Forging adds coordination constraints --- communication topology, role visibility, delegation budget --- in a layer outside it. However the model decomposes and communicates, the original environment tests still decide whether the task is done.

More precisely, write the original task as
\[
  \tau=(x,\mathcal{E},J),
  \qquad
  \mathcal{E}_c=W(\mathcal{E};c),
  \qquad
  \tau_c=(x,\mathcal{E}_c,J).
\]
Here $x$ is the user instruction, $\mathcal{E}$ the environment, $J$ the programmatic verifier. $W$ is a wrapping operator that acts \emph{only on the environment}: it does not rewrite the instruction and does not rewrite the verifier --- $x$ and $J$ are literally identical on both sides. That is the entire source of this method's safety, and the only thing separating it from ``design your own verifier''. The constraint
\[
  c=(P,V,G,B)
\]
has four components: $P$, the permission partition (which role holds which app's API); $V$, role visibility (what the Main may know about the roles); $G$, the communication graph (who may message whom); and $B$, the delegation budget.

Three notes. First, $P$ refers to the \emph{structure} of the partition --- the Main holds no tool, each specialist binds to exactly one app. Which apps those are is not a knob; it is determined by the ground truth (\S\ref{sec:select}). Second, $P$ is the most fundamental of the four: without it the Main can bypass coordination and simply do the task, and the other three become moot. Third, $J$ is unchanged word for word, but what it reads is the terminal state inside the sidecar; $W$ simultaneously guarantees that neither $J$ nor the ground truth exists anywhere on the agent's filesystem (\S\ref{sec:boundary}).

This is close in spirit to SWE-smith's principle of extending software tasks with the tests that already exist~\cite{yang2025swesmith}: reuse verification logic that is already trustworthy rather than designing fresh judging logic per task. The whole forging process reduces to three steps: identify cross-app dependencies in real scenarios, derive the roster from those dependencies, and wrap the same scenario into different coordination configurations.

\subsection{Selecting tasks from real dependencies}
\label{sec:select}

Gaia2 provides a set of everyday apps --- Calendar, Contacts, InternalContacts, Messages, Emails, Chats, Cabs, RentAFlat, Shopping and others --- inside a simulated world whose clock runs continuously and whose events fire asynchronously~\cite{meta2025are}. It decides whether a scenario was completed by checking the agent's write actions against a set of gold write actions, and ships a scripted verification mode with no model in it. This repository reads two published splits, \texttt{mini} and \texttt{adaptability}, at 160 scenarios each.

The ground truth here is not reference code, so the two mining rules port with new mechanics. The roster is still decided entirely by the task: it is the set of apps the gold writes touch, \emph{plus} any app a seam proves was read. That second clause is forced by the data --- Gaia2's oracle events record writes only, so an app the scenario merely reads leaves no trace in the ground truth and would otherwise be invisible. \texttt{AgentUserInterface}, used to report back to the user, does not count as a role, because it supplies no business information: it is this substrate's submit channel, and \S\ref{sec:yield} shows what happens to the numbers when a submit channel is counted as a collaborator.

Under that rule 111 of \texttt{mini}'s 160 scenarios and 158 of \texttt{adaptability}'s 160 genuinely need at least two apps. The rest are discarded outright rather than padded with an irrelevant role to manufacture a multi-agent shape.

\subsection{Turning a single-agent task into a collaborative one}

The wrapped environment holds one Main agent and several app-bound sub-agents. The Main sees the user goal but holds no app tool; the Contacts specialist can reach only Contacts, the Cabs specialist only Cabs. Sub-agents know only the local request the Main sent them, never the whole task.

Take \texttt{scenario\_universe\_25\_b932pi}, a mined scenario with a roster of five apps and a measured DAG depth of 3. The user asks: book a default cab ten minutes before today's earliest event, for that event's attendee, \emph{picking them up at their address}; the drop-off depends on whether the event is tagged work; message the attendee to confirm; then monitor the booking for four minutes and, if the driver cancels, delete and re-create the event without the attendee and message everyone again.

The italicised clause is the seam, and it is worth being precise about why. The gold write to \texttt{Cabs.order\_ride} carries \texttt{start\_location = "Sint-Michielsstraat 6, 3000 Leuven, Belgium"}. That string is nowhere in the user instruction --- the user said only ``their address'' --- and it is nowhere in Cabs' own initial state. It exists in Contacts. The fact therefore \emph{had} to cross an app boundary for the write to be correct, which is exactly what the provenance test detects, and no part of that inference required reading the scenario's prose. The dependency chain is the task's own:
\[
  \text{Calendar yields the earliest event, its tag and its attendee}
  \longrightarrow
  \text{Contacts yields that attendee's address}
\]
\[
  \longrightarrow
  \text{Main applies the work/non-work drop-off rule}
  \longrightarrow
  \text{Cabs orders the ride}
  \longrightarrow
  \text{Messages confirms}.
\]

No sub-agent can finish this alone: Calendar knows when and who but cannot book; Contacts knows where the attendee lives but has nothing to book with; Cabs can order a ride but knows neither the time nor the address. Gaia2 then adds an axis a static world has no way to express --- the tail of the scenario is conditional on an event four simulated minutes in the future, so the Main must stop acting and advance the clock (\S\ref{sec:boundary}) rather than guess.

This construction tests the model along three axes at once: discovering which role holds which information, ordering the subtasks correctly, and carrying the context the downstream role actually needs. But the terminal reward can only judge whether the whole thing got done; it cannot attribute one failure to one of those axes (see \S\ref{sec:verifyfirst}). The prompt remains a natural-language task, and the dependency graph exists only in the environment and the ground truth --- it is never shown to the model.

\section{The Forging Method: Four Elements}

SWE-smith's leverage is not clever bug injection. It is that it never designs a verifier: break a repository that used to work, and let the repository's own tests decide whether the fix is right~\cite{yang2025swesmith}. Taken apart, the method has only four elements: an invariant (the repo's tests), a set of operators (five ways to produce candidates), a criterion (the patch counts only if tests that used to pass go red), and a measured yield for each operator (56\%, 35\%, 40.2\%, 33.8\%, 96.9\%).

Any forging method must answer the same four questions. This section places the present design against those four elements and names the gaps.

\subsection{Invariant: inherit the verifier; if you must build one, measure it first}
\label{sec:invariant}

Forging can act at two levels: design the world and the verifier, or design only the constraints. The failure modes are not symmetric. Choose the wrong constraint and the task is too easy or too hard --- which is measurable. Design the verifier wrong and the reward measures the wrong thing --- which is invisible, because your own tests will agree with your own mistake. This design therefore touches only the latter: constraints change \emph{who may do what} and never touch \emph{what counts as done}. That is not laziness; it trades an invisible failure mode for a visible one.

The principle targets \emph{unmeasured} self-designed verifiers, not any particular technique. Language models have several defensible positions in a forging pipeline: \textbf{generating candidates} (three of SWE-smith's five operators work this way, and the issue text is model-generated too); \textbf{playing the environment} (the specialists here are real models --- swap in a scripted executor and the question ``did the Main state its request clearly?'' stops existing); and \textbf{acting as teacher where no free verifier exists}. The third needs a validation study attached: compare its judgements against a small batch of human labels and report agreement. This is not a special demand on language models --- every self-designed verifier owes the same thing, including the information-seam analysis this report uses in \S\ref{sec:yield} to filter tasks, whose error rate is likewise unmeasured.

A free verifier therefore stays the first choice, not because the alternatives are unworkable, but because that validation study is a real cost, and inheriting a verifier somebody else already validated avoids paying it.

\paragraph{Choosing a substrate.}
The invariant comes from the substrate, so choosing the substrate is the method's first step. A usable substrate must: ship a programmatic verifier (\emph{strongly} preferred, since it is free and already validated by others); be real, installable and executable; carry ground truth on at least the train split, or you are left guessing which tasks qualify; and have an action surface divisible along at least two role boundaries.

A fifth condition came out of practice on this project: the seam must be an information seam, not an operation seam. If the two sides can each work independently and merely be sequenced, that is parallel division of labour --- no fact has to cross, and adding roles is pure form. Only when the action on one side depends on a fact obtainable only from the other do delegation and communication produce a learning signal. This criterion also explains why software-repository substrates are a poor fit: they share one filesystem, no fact is private, and any partition degenerates into ``paste the file across''.

In the table, \emph{shipped} means the configuration appears in the published campaign; \emph{not built} means there is no code. ``Cells uncertifiable'' is not a yield of zero and should not be read as one: it means the acceptance criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} declined to return a verdict, because the unconstrained control on this substrate has not itself cleared the floor (\S\ref{sec:results}). SWE-smith's PR Mirror has no analogue here, because there is no PR to mirror.

One distinction has to be faced squarely: operators that change the rules produce no new tasks, only new configurations. However \texttt{partition}, \texttt{blind} and the economy target are combined, the 37 scenarios that passed the seam gate remain those same 37; $37\times4=148$ counts configurations, not tasks. SWE-smith's 50k all come from operators that edit the world itself. To make the task count actually grow, you need operators that modify the task --- and that is exactly where yield stops being free.

\texttt{combine} is the only one that would require rebuilding the verifier: Gaia2 checks a scenario's writes against \emph{its} gold action set, so merging two scenarios means deciding which writes are now legitimate, and $J_A\wedge J_B$ is not a self-consistent verifier. It has the largest potential output and sits closest to the very practice this method exists to avoid.

\subsection{Criterion: when does a candidate count}
\label{sec:criterion}

SWE-smith's criterion fits in a sentence: apply the patch, run the tests, and the candidate counts if tests that used to pass go red. Mechanical, free, judgement-free.

This design's criterion is a sandwich. Write the do-nothing floor as $r_0$, the unconstrained control as $r_{\mathrm{open}}$, and a configuration's score as $r$. The configuration counts only when
\[
  r_0 < r < r_{\mathrm{open}}
\]
and the control is not itself on the floor ($r_{\mathrm{open}}>r_0$). Landing on $r_0$ means the cell is indistinguishable from doing nothing: the reward cannot tell trying from not trying, and the gradient is zero --- whether the cause was a genuinely hard task or a broken harness. Hitting $r_{\mathrm{open}}$ means the constraint never bit, and the cell measures the base task rather than coordination. And if $r_{\mathrm{open}}$ is itself on the floor, the base task is already broken, so no knob's effect on top of it can be measured --- reading a constrained score against a control that already failed is precisely the path by which a harness fault gets published as difficulty.

Solvability is a \emph{separate} gate and is not part of this criterion. On a substrate whose ground truth is code, it is proved by running the reference solution. Gaia2 ships gold \emph{write actions} rather than a runnable script, so solvability is instead evidenced by the unconstrained control: an \texttt{open} cell that succeeds proves the scenario is reachable by an agent holding every tool. That substitution is weaker than a reference solution and this report does not pretend otherwise --- it is the direct cause of the negative result in \S\ref{sec:results}, because when the control fails, solvability and difficulty become indistinguishable.

This rule previously existed only as prose, and this repository once published a floor score as ``the partition is hard'' for two days --- it was the floor. It is now code, and its most useful property is the one exercised below: it declines to certify rather than reporting a number that looks like a result.

\subsection{Yield: forge ten, keep how many}
\label{sec:yield}

Yield is the fraction of forged candidates that are actually usable. SWE-smith measured one number per operator and used them to decide where to spend. Forging filters candidates at two points, so there are two yields: \textbf{selection} (how many scenarios in the pool qualify) and \textbf{rendering} (how many of a qualifying scenario's rendered cells are trainable). This report measures the first and finds the second not yet measurable, which is a distinction worth keeping sharp.

\subsubsection*{Selection yield: most multi-app scenarios are merely parallel}

The naive criterion is \texttt{len(roster) >= 2}. That proves ``the ground truth touched two apps''. What the method actually needs is the claim from \S\ref{sec:invariant} --- that the seam is an \emph{information} seam. If two apps each do their own work and merely need ordering, nothing is carried across, and splitting roles is division of labour rather than coordination.

On Gaia2 this is measurable with no model calls, using state provenance: take every argument value in a gold write action, and keep the scenario if some value (i)~appears in a \emph{different} app's initial state, (ii)~is absent from the target app's own state, and (iii)~did not appear in the user instruction. All three clauses are needed. Without (ii) the value might have been local all along; without (iii) the user simply told the agent, and nothing had to cross.

The two steps are the same shape one level apart: the first removes ``a channel that carries no information'', the second removes ``a second app that carries no information''. The second step is by far the more expensive one here. On \texttt{adaptability}, 99\% of scenarios are multi-app and only 19\% are seamful --- so \textbf{four fifths of the scenarios that look coordinatable are decorative}, and a pipeline that stopped at the roster check would have treated them as training data.

The splits overlap on 10 scenarios, so the corpus holds \textbf{37 unique seamful scenarios}, rendering to 148 cells with rosters of 2--7 apps, 5--16 gold writes, DAG depth 1--4 and parallel width up to 12. Per category in \texttt{mini}: adaptability 10/32, time 4/32, execution 3/32, ambiguity 0/32, search 0/32. The two zero rows are expected rather than alarming --- search and ambiguity scenarios are read-heavy, Gaia2 verifies writes only, and a fact that ends in the \emph{answer} rather than in a write is invisible to a provenance test over write arguments.

Two honest limits on this number. First, the heuristic \textbf{under-reports by construction}: it matches strings exactly, so a date reformatted in transit is invisible; values under 8 characters are ignored as too collision-prone; values present in three or more apps are treated as ambient. Second, it is a self-designed filter, and by the rule of \S\ref{sec:invariant} it therefore owes the same validation study this report demands of any self-designed judge. That study has not been run. What supports it so far is only structural: the densest seam edges are Contacts$\rightarrow$Cabs, InternalContacts$\rightarrow$Cabs and RentAFlat$\rightarrow$Cabs, which is a plausible property of the apps rather than a scatter, but plausibility is not a measurement.

\subsubsection*{Rendering yield: not measurable yet, and why that is the finding}

Applying the sandwich criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} to the campaign of \S\ref{sec:results} returns no verdict at all, for one reason: $r_{\mathrm{open}}$ has not cleared the floor. The unconstrained control succeeds on 2 of 37 scenarios and averages $0.251$ partial credit, and every constrained arm sits within $0.035$ of it.

The criterion's third clause exists precisely for this case. Reading \texttt{star-docs} at $0.253$ against a control at $0.251$ and reporting ``the partition is free'' would be the same error this repository has already made once in the other direction --- publishing a floor as a difficulty result. When the control fails, a constrained score carries no information about the constraint, because nothing establishes that the scenario was reachable to begin with.

So the rendering yield on this substrate is \textbf{undefined}, not zero, and the distinction matters for what to do next. A yield of zero would say the knobs produce unusable data. Undefined says the experiment has not run yet --- the harness, the runtime, or the base difficulty is consuming the episodes before the knobs are on trial, and \S\ref{sec:results} shows which. What this rules out is only the thing worth ruling out: reporting any of the four means in Table~\ref{tab:results} as a priced constraint.

\section{Runtime, Trajectory and Verification}

\subsection{The container boundary}
\label{sec:boundary}

Harbor packages each task as a standalone unit containing the instruction, the container environment and the tests~\cite{harbor2026tasks}. The container holding the Main agent exposes only a thin \texttt{team} interface; the Gaia2 world, the sub-agents and the final state all live on the sidecar. The Main can therefore only advance the task by delegating, and cannot bypass coordination to touch the apps directly.

One property of a rendered Gaia2 task is worth stating because it is a genuine improvement over a substrate whose world must be installed: the task carries its entire world with it, as \texttt{environment/scenario.json}. There is no dataset to download and no install step at run time, so a task directory rebuilds identically from its own contents.

The Main's verb set carries one addition this substrate forces. Gaia2's clock runs continuously and its events fire asynchronously, so a scenario can make its second half conditional on something that has not happened yet. The Main therefore holds \texttt{WAIT <seconds>}, which advances simulated time and delivers whatever notifications and user messages arrive in the interval. \emph{When to stop acting} is a coordination decision, so it belongs to the Main rather than to the harness --- and without it, an agent facing the scenario of \S\ref{sec:wrap} can only guess at a trigger four simulated minutes away.

\subsection{Reward and validity references}

The verifier does not assess whether the conversation was ``sensible'', and it does not take the model's own account of its work. Gaia2's own verdict is binary: the agent's observed write actions are checked against the scenario's gold write actions, and the scenario either passes or does not. The model may decompose and communicate however it likes; if the writes land, it earns the same verdict.

A binary verdict is a poor training signal, so this repository computes a graded one alongside it --- and that step deserves the scrutiny \S\ref{sec:invariant} demands of anything self-designed. Writing $G$ for the gold write count, $c$ for how many of them the agent performed at all, and $e$ for how many it performed with exactly the gold arguments,
\[
  r=\frac{e+\tfrac{1}{2}(c-e)}{G}.
\]
An exact match scores fully, a right-tool-wrong-arguments match scores half: an agent that sent the correct message to the wrong number did some of the work, and scoring it zero is the all-or-nothing signal again. Two properties keep this honest. It is \textbf{deterministic and model-free} --- recomputed from trajectories on disk, so no judge can move it --- and it is \textbf{a floor beneath the official verdict rather than a replacement for it}: it never certifies a scenario the verifier failed. It is nonetheless a reward this project designed, so it is reported as a diagnostic and never as the acceptance criterion. Every verdict in \S\ref{sec:results} comes from Gaia2's verifier.

To confirm the reward means anything, each configuration needs three references. The first is a demonstration that the scenario is solvable at all. The second is the unconstrained control, giving a single agent every app. The third is the do-nothing baseline, measuring what already scores when no action is taken. Only when the constrained group sits between the solvable ceiling and the no-action floor, and produces a non-degenerate distribution across instances, is the task set suitable for RL.

On this substrate the first and third references are the ones still missing, and \S\ref{sec:results} is what that costs. Gaia2 ships gold write actions rather than a runnable reference solution, so the control is doing double duty as the solvability proof --- and when the control fails, there is nothing left to distinguish ``hard scenario'' from ``broken harness''. A do-nothing probe has not been run on Gaia2 at all. Both are cheap and neither has been done; that is the honest statement of where this delivery stands.

\section{Difficulty Control and Scaling}

\subsection{Which directions difficulty moves along}

Difficulty varies along four directions. First, information visibility: the Main may read each role's capability documentation, or see only role names. Second, dependency structure: a task can grow from a two-step sequential dependency to three apps and multi-branch dependencies. Third, communication constraints, including the delegation budget and the star, chain and tree topologies. Fourth, the operational scale of the task itself --- pages to traverse, entities involved, state modifications required.

These have to be added one layer at a time. A valid difficulty knob must satisfy two conditions: the reference solution still passes reliably, and the model's reward or failure structure changes reproducibly against the previous layer. A topology that merely makes the task unexecutable produces an environment fault, not higher difficulty --- which is how chain got kept out of the delivery (\S\ref{sec:operators}).

\subsection{Batch generation: the free half}

The forging pipeline is four commands, and three of them call no model. \texttt{gaia2\_fetch} pulls a published split. \texttt{gaia2\_admission\_probe} reads each scenario's gold write actions and initial app state, derives the roster, and runs the provenance test that keeps the seamful ones. \texttt{gaia2\_render\_probe} turns those scenarios and the constraint configurations into cell specifications, and \texttt{forge.gaia2.cli render} writes Harbor task directories. All are static analysis plus file writes, and finish on a laptop in minutes.

The reason they are free is written in the equation of \S\ref{sec:wrap}: $W$ acts only on the environment, and $x$ and $J$ are unchanged word for word. Multiplying along the configuration dimension therefore creates no new design work --- one more visibility setting is one more pass of writing task directories. Across the two splits read, 37 seamful scenarios and the four-cell grid produce \textbf{148 cells} from a single command.

The real question is how many of those 148 cells can ship. The next two sections are the two costs, both downstream of rendering --- and on this substrate the first one changed shape entirely.

\subsection{Cost one: the reference solution moved, it did not disappear}
\label{sec:refcost}

On a substrate whose ground truth is reference \emph{code}, this section was about authorship: a Harbor task ships a \texttt{solution/} to prove it is solvable, those paths were written one at a time, and the count of hand-written solutions was the hard limit on how many rendered directories could ship.

Gaia2 removes that cost outright, and it is the strongest practical argument for the substrate. Ground truth is a list of gold write actions, present for every scenario in the split, so nothing has to be authored per task to know what ``done'' means: the acceptance target for all 37 scenarios arrives free with the data.

What it does not supply is a \emph{runnable} reference. Gold write actions describe the destination, not a route an agent can be made to follow --- and a Harbor \texttt{solution/} has to execute. So the cost did not vanish; it moved from ``write a reference path per task'' to ``no cheap proof that any given scenario is reachable''. \S\ref{sec:criterion} noted the consequence and \S\ref{sec:results} pays for it: with no reference solution to run, the unconstrained control is the only available solvability evidence, and this campaign's control failed on 35 of 37 scenarios. That leaves the central question --- are these scenarios hard, or is this runtime broken? --- formally open.

Two things would close it, and neither is expensive. A \textbf{gold replay} agent that issues the recorded write actions directly would establish the ceiling with no model calls at all, and would fail loudly if the harness rather than the scenario is at fault. A \textbf{do-nothing probe}, the analogue of the floor measurement this project already runs elsewhere, would establish the bottom of the range. Together they would turn every number in \S\ref{sec:results} from suggestive into readable. That they have not been run is the largest gap in this delivery.

\subsection{Cost two: verification, and it grows linearly in configurations}

Verification cannot be free, because validity is a property of the rollout distribution rather than of a single draw (\S\ref{sec:yield}). A cell's yield needs five replicates before it counts as measured, and the arithmetic follows directly: 37 scenarios $\times$ 4 cells $\times$ 5 seeds $=740$ rollouts, of which the campaign reported here has run \textbf{148} --- one seed, and the evidence file says so in its own note.

Compute is not the wall. Two other things are. First, the ceiling is API rate rather than compute: the campaign runs at three concurrent episodes because the organisation's token-per-minute ceiling makes more counter-productive, so wall-clock is set by the rate limit and not by the work. Second, this term grows linearly in the number of configurations --- every new knob has to be re-run across every scenario with a full set of seeds before anyone knows whether that knob is difficulty or a fault. This is exactly the argument for measuring yield before scaling out, and \S\ref{sec:results} is what it looks like when the argument is ignored in the other direction: 148 rollouts were spent on four knobs before anyone had established that the control could finish a scenario. The four extra cells per scenario were not the wrong idea, but they were the wrong \emph{order}.

\subsection{The ceiling: 37 scenarios, but only 12 seam structures}
\label{sec:ceiling}

Even with both costs solved, the source itself has a hard ceiling, and it is well below 37.

The 37 seam-bearing scenarios are not 37 independent samples. Grouped by their seam signature --- the set of \emph{(source app $\rightarrow$ target app)} edges the provenance test found --- they collapse into \textbf{12 distinct structures}, and three of those cover 25 of the 37 (Table~\ref{tab:combos}).

The abstract's ``configuration count is not task count'' needs one more turn of the screw here: \textbf{task count is not structure count either}. Two of the five published capability categories contributed nothing at all, for a reason \S\ref{sec:yield} gives: a fact that ends in the answer rather than in a write is invisible to a provenance test. Widening the corpus therefore means either fetching the remaining splits --- an unmeasured but cheap extrapolation --- or extending the seam test to read-bearing evidence, which the current rule cannot see. What is missing is not a knob. It is source scenarios, and a seam test with a wider aperture.

\subsection{The admission test: what the method asks of a database}
\label{sec:substrate}

Gaia2 is an argument to this method, not a premise of it. The method asks four things of a database, and dropping any one collapses it back into ``design your own verifier'' --- which \S\ref{sec:invariant} argues is forbidden by the very reason the method exists:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{A programmatic verifier you did not write.} It is what makes the reward batch-computable, and what excuses you from ever defending a judgement about ``how well they collaborated''. Its value is precisely that somebody else already validated it.
\item \textbf{Real, executable, installable.} Not a description of a world. A database you cannot \texttt{pip install} or \texttt{docker pull} is one you will finish by building --- and then you designed it, and requirement 1 is gone with it.
\item \textbf{Ground truth per task.} It serves two purposes: deriving the roster (the roles come from whatever the ground truth touched, not from the designer's hand) and proving solvability. Gaia2 supplies the first in full and the second only partly, since gold write actions describe a destination rather than a route --- \S\ref{sec:refcost} is what that costs.
\item \textbf{A tool boundary that can be cut.} The permission partition is the most fundamental of the four constraints (\S\ref{sec:wrap}): if the tools cannot be split by role, the Main can bypass coordination and finish alone, and the other three become moot.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Admission is necessary and not sufficient.} A database can clear all four and still yield nothing, because none of the four measures how \emph{deep} the seams run: it may ship a flawless verifier and have every task collapse onto a single role. That is why seam analysis (\S\ref{sec:select}) is a measurement rather than an assumption, and Gaia2 makes the point emphatically --- 99\% of the \texttt{adaptability} split is multi-app and only 19\% is seamful. The seams are also where substrate choice stops being free: SWE-smith has the strongest verifier on the list and scaled on the strength of requirement 1~\cite{yang2025swesmith}, but its seams are code modules, so partitioning it yields a software-engineering task with extra agents rather than an orchestration task.

\textbf{Admission does not measure difficulty either}, and this delivery is the demonstration. Gaia2 cleared all four requirements comfortably, its seam yield was measured rather than assumed, and 148 cells still produced no certifiable training data --- because the requirements say nothing about whether a runtime can carry an agent through a scenario of that length. The admission test screens the \emph{database}. Nothing in it screens the \emph{harness}, and \S\ref{sec:results} argues that is where this campaign was actually lost.

\textbf{How far this generalises, stated honestly.} Nothing in the method is Gaia2-shaped: the admission test is a property of a database, the roster is read off whatever the ground truth touches, the constraints act on an access surface, and the validity rule (\S\ref{sec:criterion}) needs only a score, a floor and a control. That is an argument from construction --- checkable by reading it --- and it now has one piece of empirical support beyond a single case: both mining rules ported to a substrate whose ground truth has a completely different form, from reference \emph{code} to gold \emph{write actions}, and produced a measured seam distribution on the far side. What has \textbf{not} ported is an end-to-end priced knob, because no campaign on this substrate has yet cleared its own control. The order for scaling up is therefore: establish a ceiling and a floor on the current substrate (\S\ref{sec:refcost}), re-run the grid at five seeds, and only then widen sideways --- rather than piling more configurations onto scenarios nobody has shown to be finishable.

Main and specialists are both \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}, at model parity by construction. \textbf{One seed.}

The acceptance criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} refuses this table by its third clause --- $r_{\mathrm{open}}$ is not above the floor --- and that refusal is the result being reported. Publishing ``the partition is free, and a delegation budget helps'' from these numbers would repeat, with better tooling and more rollouts, exactly the error \S\ref{sec:invariant} exists to prevent.

Three pieces of evidence show the ordering is noise rather than signal.

\textbf{First, the control does not behave like a ceiling.} On \textbf{6 of 37 scenarios a constrained arm outscored the unconstrained one} --- an agent holding every tool did worse than one that had to delegate blind. On \texttt{..\_24\_srcjx4}, the widest scenario in the corpus, the control surrendered having issued zero actions and scored $0.0$, while the \texttt{discovery} arm covered half the gold writes. A control that loses to its own blinded condition is not measuring difficulty.

The honest summary is therefore short. The mining ported and produced a measured seam distribution; the runtime ran 148 cells end to end and logged full trajectories; and the acceptance criterion, applied to those cells, returned \emph{no verdict} --- correctly. What this campaign priced is not a constraint. It is the runtime, and the price was 49\% of the rollouts.

\section{Discussion and Extensions}

\subsection{Shore up the existing conclusions first}
\label{sec:verifyfirst}
\label{sec:next}

The ordering of the next steps follows directly from \S\ref{sec:results}, and none of the expensive options come first.

\textbf{Establish the ceiling and the floor.} A gold-replay agent that issues the recorded write actions directly costs no model calls and answers the question the campaign could not: is a scenario that the control failed actually reachable in this runtime? A do-nothing probe, which this project already runs on other substrates, supplies the other end of the range. Neither has been run on Gaia2, and until both are, no cell can be certified and no knob priced.

\textbf{Fix the runtime before adding anything to it.} Forty malformed tool calls and 33 environment stops are 49\% of the corpus, and they are addressable engineering rather than research: the call syntax can be constrained rather than requested, and the clock budget can be measured against each scenario's horizon. Every rollout spent on a fifth knob before that is a rollout spent measuring the harness.

\textbf{Separate the judge from the population.} Running both verifiers over the same trajectories costs one pass and would turn Table~\ref{tab:judges} from an uninterpretable gap into a measurement of judge strictness.

\textbf{Only then, seeds.} Five replicates over 37 scenarios and 4 cells is 740 rollouts, and it is worth paying once the control clears the floor and not before.

There is a second gap of a different kind. \textbf{The method is presented as substrate-independent}, and this delivery is the first real test of that claim, since Gaia2's ground truth has a completely different form from reference code. The mining rules ported: roster derivation and the seam test both produced measured distributions on the far side, which is genuine evidence and is the strongest result in this report. What did not port is an end-to-end priced knob --- not because the method failed to transfer, but because the campaign never established its own control. The generality claim is therefore now partly measured and partly still an argument from construction, and this report marks the boundary rather than letting the successful half stand for the whole.

Beyond that, the current reward reliably judges whether the scenario was completed but cannot separate failures of decomposition, role selection and communication. The pivot annotation of Table~\ref{tab:pivots} is a first step toward attribution --- it is deterministic and model-free, and it already distinguishes ``never issued a parseable call'' from ``called the right tool with the wrong argument''. It is not yet capability attribution, and it should not enter a reward until its accuracy has been measured against human labels, by the same rule this report applies to any self-designed judge.

\subsection{From spatial constraints to temporal ones}

The current work changes the spatial structure of collaboration: who holds which tools, who knows what, along which topology messages may travel. The next stage can add a time axis to the same environment, so that information is not handed over all at once at the start but appears when the agent asks for it or when the task reaches a particular state. That would train hidden-requirement discovery and dynamic replanning under user intervention, without giving up terminal-state verification.

A minimal version generates paired samples from the same complete task. The control group still receives the full instruction; the experimental group receives a deliberately incomplete but realistically phrased request, with the contact scope, date or compatibility preference held in hidden user state in the sidecar. The Main can obtain those constraints only by noticing the gap and asking the user. Both groups share an initial world and a final verifier, so there is no need to score ``was that a good question?'' separately: if the key question never gets asked, the agent cannot reliably reach the correct terminal state; and asking about everything burns a limited interaction budget.

User intervention can serve as the next difficulty layer. After the agent has done part of the work, simulate the user adding a changed scope or a new restriction, requiring the Main to update the plan, keep the results that remain valid, and pass the new constraint to the relevant roles. Two cases must be distinguished here: if the added information was always part of the original goal and merely unstated, the final judge can stay unchanged; if the user genuinely changed the goal, the new terminal state needs an equally trustworthy environment check, and cannot be judged from the conversation alone.

This temporal extension can be written as
\[
  \tau_{c,h}=(x_0,\mathcal{E}_c,U_h,\Gamma_h,J),
\]
where $U_h$ is hidden user state existing only in the sidecar and $\Gamma_h$ decides when information is asked for, revealed or modified. The project's most important boundary still has to hold: hidden state must never enter the Main's filesystem, the interaction record must not be forgeable by the agent, and the final reward must continue to come from replayable environment state. Only once paired experiments reliably separate the scores of full instruction, hidden requirement and mid-run intervention should it enter a formal curriculum.

\section{Conclusion}

The key to this method is not inventing new tasks out of nothing. It is finding coordination dependencies inside real tasks that already exist, then making those dependencies explicit through permission and information allocation. The Main must plan and delegate, the sub-agents can only do local work, and the verdict still comes from Gaia2's own write-action verifier.

Two things in this report are established. The mining rules \textbf{ported to a substrate whose ground truth has an entirely different form}, from reference code to gold write actions, and produced measured seam distributions on two splits: 17 of 160 and 30 of 160, against a multi-app rate of 69\% and 99\%. And the pipeline runs end to end --- 37 scenarios mined, 148 cells rendered, 148 rollouts executed with full trajectories, three tasks packaged as container-ready Harbor directories.

One thing is not. \textbf{Whether difficulty can be tuned along these directions remains unproved, and this campaign moved no closer to proving it.} The four cells sit within $0.035$ of each other, the unconstrained control succeeded on 2 of 37 scenarios, and 49\% of rollouts ended on a malformed call or an environment stop. The acceptance criterion declined to certify any cell, which is the criterion working: it is designed to withhold a verdict exactly when a control has not cleared the floor, and it did.

What comes next is therefore not scaling up. It is establishing the two references the campaign lacked --- a gold-replay ceiling and a do-nothing floor --- and repairing the half of the runtime that is losing episodes to syntax and to the clock. Only then do seeds, further splits and new knobs buy anything. A forge that cannot yet finish its own control has no business adding a fifth constraint, and the most valuable thing this delivery produced is the evidence that says so.
